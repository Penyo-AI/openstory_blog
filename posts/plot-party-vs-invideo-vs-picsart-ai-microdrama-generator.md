## Use One Three-Episode Brief for a Fair Comparison

A single beautiful clip does not show whether a tool can sustain a story. A three-episode pilot introduces the extra demands: the lead returns in the same costume, a prop reappears, and a cliffhanger must connect to the next opening.

Use a small romance mystery with two adult leads and one location:

1. **Episode one: the arrival.** Elena appears at Marcus's mansion in the rain. He recognizes her. She says, "You told everyone I died three years ago."
2. **Episode two: the evidence.** Inside the doorway, Elena shows a photograph. Marcus denies knowing who sent it. A distinctive watch links him to the image.
3. **Episode three: the reversal.** A phone message reveals that someone inside the house expected Elena. End before showing who it was.

Prepare the same approved portraits, wardrobe references, location image and script for every platform. Choose the same target duration and vertical format. Set a generation budget in advance and count all retries, including attempts that never reach the edit.

If a workflow needs another application for assembly, use it and record the additional time and cost. That is useful production evidence. A fair comparison measures the finished job with the actual tools required to complete it.

## What to Evaluate Beyond the First Clip

### Recurring Cast and Prop Continuity

Compare Elena's face, hair and dress across wide shots and close-ups. Check Marcus's watch, the umbrella and the photograph. A face can remain recognizable while wardrobe and props change enough to break the scene.

Use the [character-sheet comparison](./nano-banana-2-vs-gpt-image-2-5-vs-midjourney.md) to prepare references, then test the same difficult camera angles in each workflow. Count the corrections needed to preserve the approved design.

### Acting and Dialogue

The reveal needs a reaction, not just a technically synchronized mouth. Test whether Marcus looks shocked before Elena speaks, whether the right character delivers the line, and whether the camera stays with the intended subject.

Start with one spoken line per shot. Compare pronunciation, pacing, voice consistency and background sound in the assembled sequence. Add a second language only after the original scene works.

### Shot Planning and Editability

Watch the scene without sound. The entrance, reverse angle and evidence insert should still make spatial sense. Screen direction, eyelines and lighting need to agree between cuts.

Then request one revision: change Marcus's reaction while preserving Elena, the doorway and the existing timing. Measure how much must be regenerated and whether the approved version is easy to recover. This reveals more about a production workflow than a model list does.

### Cost and Review Time

Record credits spent, editor time and the number of accepted shots. Use the same acceptance criteria across tools. Avoid calling a visual result "98% consistent" unless you have a defined scoring method, a sample and the underlying results.

| Pilot measure | How to record it |
|---|---|
| Accepted footage | Seconds retained in the approved episode |
| Generation efficiency | Approved shots divided by generated attempts |
| Continuity repairs | Number of shots revised for identity, wardrobe or props |
| Human effort | Time spent planning, reviewing, editing and mastering |
| Production cost | Credits plus attributable software, labor and delivery costs |

## A Copyable 9:16 Microdrama Scene Prompt

Attach the approved character and location files in [Plot Party Studio](https://plotparty.ai/home?tab=studio), then use this as a scene brief. Replace the character labels with references to the actual files. Other platforms may use a different reference syntax.

```text
Plan a three-shot vertical 9:16 microdrama sequence.
Use the attached Elena, Marcus and mansion references.
Keep both adult characters' identities and costumes consistent.

Shot 1: Elena stands outside a luxury glass mansion in heavy rain.
She wears the approved white evening gown. Medium-wide frame,
cinematic reflections, doorway visible to establish geography.

Shot 2: Reverse angle. Marcus opens the mahogany door holding
a black umbrella. His expression moves from confusion to shock.
Keep the watch on his left wrist and preserve the entrance layout.

Shot 3: Close-up of Elena as she says,
"You told everyone I died three years ago."
Controlled, tearful delivery. Rain ambience and distant thunder.
Keep speech clear. End on the unresolved reaction.

Create the storyboard first. List references and dialogue per shot.
Generate shots separately where needed for the selected model.
```

Approve the storyboard before generating motion. The prompt is a production brief, not a promise that every model can deliver three shots and dialogue in one request. Use the supported controls for the chosen model and check the cut after assembly.

For model selection, the [MiniMax H3 vs Seedance 2.5 guide](./minimax-h3-vs-seedance-2-5-comparison.md) discusses reference and dialogue workflows. Choose based on the shot you need rather than switching models after every unsuccessful attempt.
